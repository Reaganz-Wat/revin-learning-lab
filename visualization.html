Variance — Why Some Columns Are More Spread Out

All four columns in the CSV have different variances. This is exactly why a model can't treat them equally — and why normalization matters.

📁 data/fundamentals_data.csv  ·  📓 Notebook 02
σ² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / n
Subtract the mean from each value, square it (so negatives become positive), then average all of those squares. Larger spread → larger variance.

Standard Deviation — Back to the Original Units

Variance is in "squared units" (scores²), which is hard to interpret. Taking its square root gives the standard deviation — back in the same unit as your data.

📁 data/fundamentals_data.csv → student_score  ·  📓 Notebook 03
σ = √(σ²) → 68% of data within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ

Z-Score — How Far Is Each Student From Average?

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. z=0 is exactly average. z=2 is two standard deviations above. |z|>3 is usually flagged as an outlier.

📁 data/fundamentals_data.csv → student_score  ·  📓 Notebook 05
z = (x − μ) / σ → z = (score − 69.30) / 11.10
Using the real mean and std from student_score. Green bars = normal range. Amber = mild outlier (|z|>2). Red = strong outlier (|z|>3).

RevIN — Reversible Instance Normalization

Pick any 20-day window from the real stock data. RevIN normalizes it with that window's own mean and std, the model predicts in normalized space, then RevIN brings the predictions back to dollar prices.

📁 data/stock_sample.csv — 120 real trading days  ·  📓 Notebooks 08 & 09
NORMALIZE: x̂ = (x − μ) / (σ + ε) MODEL: ŷ_norm = model(x̂) DENORMALIZE: ŷ = ŷ_norm × σ + μ
The stored μ and σ come from the input window itself — not from the whole training set. This is what makes it work across regimes.
